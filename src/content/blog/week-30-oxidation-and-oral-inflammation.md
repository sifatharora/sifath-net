This week we studied **oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions** in chemistry — reactions where electrons transfer between substances. It sounded abstract until I connected it to gum disease and inflammation.

## Free Radicals and Oxidative Stress

In living systems, some redox reactions produce **free radicals**, highly reactive molecules with unpaired electrons. In small amounts, they're part of normal immune defense. In large amounts, they can damage cells and tissues — a state called oxidative stress.

Chronic gum inflammation can increase oxidative stress in the mouth and beyond. That links back to Week 25, when I wrote about oral bacteria and whole-body health. Redox chemistry helps explain *how* local inflammation can create wider effects.

## Antioxidants Push Back

Antioxidants are substances that help neutralize free radicals. Some come from saliva and the body's own defenses; others come from foods rich in vitamins and plant compounds. A balanced diet doesn't replace brushing and flossing, but it can support the chemical systems that manage inflammation.

## Why Dentists Watch the Gums

Healthy gums aren't only about avoiding bleeding. They're about keeping inflammatory chemistry under control. When plaque bacteria trigger ongoing immune responses, redox imbalance can rise, tissues break down, and the mouth-body connection becomes more dangerous.

## Final Thoughts

Week 30 made inflammation feel less mysterious. It's not just "swelling" — it's a network of chemical signals and electron-transfer reactions. For dentistry, that means prevention is powerful: control plaque early, and you reduce the chemical cascade before it spreads.

Chemistry keeps showing me that oral health is whole-body science in miniature.
