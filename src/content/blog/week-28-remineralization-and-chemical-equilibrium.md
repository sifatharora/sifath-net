This week in chemistry we studied **chemical equilibrium** — the idea that some reactions go both directions until they reach a balance. That concept completely changed how I understand enamel repair.

## Enamel Is Always Changing

Tooth enamel may look solid and permanent, but at the microscopic level it's in constant exchange with saliva. Calcium and phosphate ions move out of enamel during acid attacks (**demineralization**) and move back in when conditions improve (**remineralization**).

In equilibrium language, the system looks like this:

**enamel minerals ⇌ dissolved ions in saliva**

Acid pushes the reaction toward dissolving minerals. Neutral pH and mineral-rich saliva push it back toward rebuilding enamel.

## Early Damage Can Reverse

This is why white spot lesions matter. In the earliest stage, enamel structure is weakened but not fully destroyed. If you restore pH and supply minerals (through saliva, diet, and fluoride), remineralization can catch up.

Once a cavity breaks through into dentin, the balance has shifted too far — and the tooth needs professional treatment. Chemistry makes that distinction clearer: reversible equilibrium versus irreversible damage.

## Fluoride Tips the Balance

Fluoride helps by forming fluorapatite, which is more acid-resistant than regular hydroxyapatite. It also encourages minerals to redeposit into weakened enamel. In other words, fluoride doesn't just "coat" teeth — it changes the equilibrium in favor of repair and protection.

## Final Thoughts

Week 28 taught me that oral health is dynamic. Every day, your mouth is running chemical reactions that either protect or weaken enamel. The goal isn't perfection — it's keeping the balance on the side of remineralization as often as possible.

That's a powerful idea for future dentists and for anyone who wants to keep their smile strong.
